# TABASC threat model

## Scope and assets

This model covers the current TABASC application routes in `client/routes/` and the four contracts in `contracts/`: `RoleRegistry`, `ProductContract`, `TrackingContract`, and `ReputationContract`. The HTTP API is mounted under `/api` (`client/server.js`): authentication/profile, product registration and lookup, shipment/buyer/recall operations, reputation/feedback, QR, admin, public verification, and health. Current project configuration uses a local development chain; deployment to a public network is out of scope.

Assets to protect:

- Account identity records, role and activation state, bearer tokens, wallet addresses, encrypted wallet keystores, the JWT-signing secret, and the wallet-keystore passphrase.
- The on-chain role registry; product registration, ownership/custody and batch data hashes; shipment history, care attestations, buyer assignments, and recalls.
- Inspector scores, consumer feedback, stakeholder reputation, restrictions, and reputation configuration.
- MongoDB product, batch, user, event, audit, and `PendingSync` records that support application queries and synchronization.
- Participation-agreement acceptance records and the confidentiality, availability, and integrity of the API and its signing/deployment keys.

## Actors and trust assumptions

| Actor | Intended authority and trust assumption |
|---|---|
| Admin | The deployer is the initial on-chain administrator. Admins approve accounts and supply-chain roles, configure reputation, and may suspend accounts. The model assumes this authority and its signing key are controlled and used appropriately. |
| Inspector | An approved inspector records quality/compliance scores, finalizes scores, and can recall products or apply a penalty after a recall. The model assumes the inspector is qualified, independent of the product participants, and reports honestly. |
| Farmer | An approved farmer registers batches and begins custody. The model assumes product facts and registration metadata are accurate. |
| Distributor | An approved distributor records transit/delivery and self-reports storage care when handing custody to a retailer. |
| Retailer | An approved retailer records shelf/sale progression and proposes the buyer. The model assumes the retailer does not select a controlled or fictitious buyer. |
| Consumer | An active consumer can confirm a buyer assignment proposed for their wallet and submit feedback. The model assumes the account represents a real, independent purchaser; the code does not establish that fact. |

Roles are represented in both application user records and the on-chain `RoleRegistry`. API access uses a JWT and the stored active account; transaction routes additionally check the caller's on-chain role or custody where implemented. A role label or agreement acceptance is not proof of a real-world identity, honest conduct, or accurate sensor data.

## Attacks and defenses

| Attack | How it works in this system | Current defense (code) | Residual risk | Possible mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Retailer proposes a fake buyer to inflate its own score | A retailer may propose a separate consumer account/wallet it controls for a sold batch. That account can confirm and submit favorable feedback. The API requires an active consumer account and a wallet different from the retailer's, but does not prove that the consumer is independent or bought the goods. The Solidity contract only requires a nonzero buyer different from the retailer, then requires that wallet to confirm. | `TrackingContract.recordBuyer` and `confirmBuyer`; API `POST /api/tracking/:id/buyer` and `POST /api/tracking/:id/buyer/confirm`; `ReputationContract.submitFeedback` ties feedback to the confirmed buyer and a sold product. The baseline simulation (`docs/evidence/baseline-comparison.md`) gives the colluder a 10/10 controlled-buyer rating: it ranks last under TABASC (mean 5,144.87 bps) but is not restricted; under plain average it ranks first. This is simulated behavior, not field evidence. | A controlled consumer account can meet the protocol checks and create genuine-looking on-chain feedback. Under the cited simulation settings, poor scores reduce reputation but do not cross the restriction threshold. | Add a verifiable purchase/fulfillment receipt or independent buyer evidence; monitor retailer-to-buyer relationships and repeated wallet overlap; review anomalous score histories; calibrate restriction thresholds against validated data. |
| One buyer repeatedly reviews the same actor | The same buyer can be selected on multiple distinct sold batches and provide one rating for each, repeatedly influencing the reputation of a recurring participant. | `ReputationContract.submitFeedback` permits only one feedback record per product (`AlreadyReviewed`); `TrackingContract.recordBuyer` records only one buyer per product and `confirmBuyer` is one-time. `POST /api/reputation/reviews` checks buyer identity, sold/recalled state, and existing feedback. | There is no on-chain or API limit on how many different products one buyer can review for the same stakeholder, nor a per-buyer influence cap. A colluding retailer can repeatedly select a controlled buyer across batches. | Detect repeated buyer/stakeholder pairs and related wallets, limit or cap correlated ratings in the scoring model, and require independent transaction evidence. |
| Colluding or compromised inspector | An inspector can submit favorable/hostile inspection values, finalize reputation, recall a batch, or apply a penalty after recall. Collusion with a participant or loss of the inspector key can misuse those permissions. | `ReputationContract.recordInspection` requires the inspector role, rejects an inspector who is a product participant, validates score bounds, and allows one inspection per product. `finalizeScore` also requires the inspector role and finalizes each stakeholder/product once. API `POST /api/reputation/score` verifies the role and participant status. `TrackingContract.recall` requires the inspector role; `ReputationContract.applyPenalty` requires a recalled product and permits one penalty per participant/product. Actions emit on-chain events. | There is no second-inspector approval, independent evidence check, or cryptographic sensor attestation. A valid inspector can still make a dishonest judgment; one inspection per batch makes a bad value immutable absent a new batch. | Require multiple independent inspectors or a review threshold for consequential actions; provide auditable evidence and appeals; monitor inspector scoring and recall patterns; secure and rotate inspector credentials. |
| Sybil accounts (many fake consumers) | An attacker creates multiple identities to act as buyers or influence feedback. Email aliases and accounts controlled by one person can appear distinct to the application. | `POST /api/auth/register` requires an email and agreement acceptance, enforces unique email/wallet records through the user model, and creates new accounts inactive/pending. Login requires an active account. Admin activation and role approval are behind `requireAdmin`. `client/server.js` applies IP-based rate limits to registration/login and a general API limit. | No identity proof, purchaser verification, or uniqueness check across real people is implemented. Distributed IPs, multiple email addresses, or compromised accounts can evade simple rate limits. Admin activation is a trust dependency. | Add privacy-preserving identity or verified-purchase checks, risk-based account review, and cross-account relationship detection; tune limits with trusted proxy configuration and operational monitoring. |
| MongoDB tampering versus on-chain truth | A database writer can alter or delete cached user/product/batch/event data, which may affect API display, reconciliation inputs, or application decisions. The chain retains its own records, but not every MongoDB field is committed on-chain. | `ProductContract` stores a registration hash; `GET /api/verify/:blockchainId` recomputes the canonical product hash and reports whether it matches. `products.js` also calls `requireProductDataMatch` before placing a product OnShelf or Sold, while custody and current chain state are read from contracts by `supplyChain.js`. Blockchain-success/database-failure paths create `PendingSync`; `client/scripts/reconcile.js` retries those recorded operations and records run results. | Hash verification covers the canonical product fields, not every MongoDB field or the truth of the original claims. `PendingSync` and reconciliation payloads are themselves in MongoDB; reconciliation is a retry mechanism for known pending writes, not a full integrity scan or proof against a malicious database administrator. Some API views and audit history depend on MongoDB. | Restrict database credentials and network access, use backups and immutable/externally retained audit logs, monitor chain-to-database discrepancies, and periodically rebuild/verify cached state from chain events and contract reads. Treat DB audit entries and pending payloads as operational evidence, not chain truth. |
| Admin or deployer key compromise | A stolen deployer/admin key can make privileged contract calls or, through the application admin account, alter roles and system operations. It could grant roles (including another admin), change reputation configuration, suspend participants, and enable privileged account management. | `RoleRegistry.grantRole`/`revokeRole` and reputation administration functions require the on-chain admin role. `admin.js` applies `protect` and `requireAdmin`, which checks the user role, requires its wallet to match the configured deployer account, and checks `RoleRegistry.isAdmin`. Deactivation/revocation is blocked while the account holds active custody (`assertNoActiveCustody`). | These checks all trust the same configured deployer authority; they do not mitigate compromise of that key. The contracts do not implement a multisig or time delay, and an authorized key can call the contracts directly outside the API. | Move privileged authority to a hardware-protected multisig, add a timelock and independent approvals for role/config changes, use separate least-privilege keys, and prepare monitored revocation/recovery procedures. |
| Replay or front-running of transactions on a public chain | An observer could watch pending calls and try to reorder, copy, or race actions, potentially causing a stale transaction to fail or delaying a user's action. A public mempool also exposes transaction intent and timing. | There is no explicit application-level commit/reveal or anti-front-running mechanism. Contract functions check `msg.sender`, role, current custodian, product status and recall state; `updateShipment` only accepts the next lifecycle state, and buyer confirmation must come from the selected wallet and can occur once. These state checks constrain who can perform or repeat an action, but are not general replay or ordering protection. | The current local development configuration does not establish public-network behavior. For a public chain, transaction visibility, inclusion ordering, congestion, chain reorganizations, and network-specific replay properties remain risks; the contracts do not define a cross-chain domain or deadline for most actions. | Before public deployment, select and document chain/replay protections, review signed-transaction and chain-ID handling, add deadlines or intent commitments where ordering matters, and assess MEV/private transaction options. |
| Registration agreement treated as technical enforcement | A registrant can accept the checkbox/terms without being the claimed person or complying later. Acceptance is a legal/organizational deterrent, not proof of conduct or a mechanism that makes subsequent claims true. | `auth.js` `POST /register` requires `agreementAccepted === true` and the current version and stores the version, SHA-256 terms hash, and acceptance time. The agreement (`client/policies/stakeholderAgreement.js`) requires accurate information and account security; admin routes can deactivate accounts or revoke supply-chain roles subject to custody checks. | Acceptance is self-attested. There is no technical verification of identity, ongoing compliance, or that product/inspection/sensor claims are truthful. Legal remedies and enforcement depend on external processes. | Keep the agreement as a deterrent and documented policy, but pair it with identity/purchase verification, role-specific evidence, monitoring, investigation and appeal procedures, and clear key-compromise reporting. |
| Backend or server compromise (custodial signing) | The server stores each user's wallet keystore in encrypted form (`User.walletKeystore`). Transaction handlers load the user's keystore and `sendFromWallet` decrypts it and signs transactions on that user's behalf. `WALLET_PASSPHRASE` and `JWT_SECRET` are loaded from the server environment via `client/config/env.js`; the same passphrase is used to encrypt and decrypt user keystores. | Keystores are encrypted with `web3.eth.accounts.encrypt` and decrypted only for signing through `unlockWallet`/`sendFromWallet` in `client/blockchain/wallets.js`. Routes authenticate with `protect`, enforce application roles, and transaction paths perform on-chain role/custody checks where implemented. `client/server.js` has 10-login/register-attempt and 300-general-API-request per-IP 15-minute limits. These controls limit normal API access but do not protect secrets from a fully compromised server. | Whoever controls the server and can access its environment and stored user keystores can obtain the shared keystore passphrase and sign as any user whose keystore is available, bypassing application role checks by changing or bypassing the server. Encryption at rest does not isolate the key from the server that decrypts it. | Move signing to user-controlled wallets or an isolated signing service/HSM with per-user authorization; isolate secrets from application processes and database backups; apply least privilege, host hardening, monitoring, and key-rotation/recovery procedures. |
| Stolen or leaked JWT or account credentials | A stolen account password can be used to log in and obtain a JWT; a stolen bearer token can be replayed as that account until it expires or the account is rejected by middleware. | `auth.js` issues JWTs with the account ID and `config.jwtExpiresIn` (`JWT_EXPIRES_IN`, validated as a duration such as `2h` or `30m` in `client/config/env.js`). The `protect` middleware in `client/routes/auth.js` verifies the signature with `JWT_SECRET`, reloads the user on each protected request, and rejects missing, invalid, expired, missing-user, or inactive-user tokens. Admin deactivation sets `isActive` false, so subsequent protected requests using existing tokens are rejected. Login is rate-limited in `client/server.js`. | Individual JWT revocation is **not implemented**: there is no token denylist, session-version check, or per-token revocation record. An unexpired token remains usable until expiration unless the account is deactivated; if reactivated before expiry, the old token can work again. Changing a password does not invalidate already-issued JWTs. Leaked credentials can also be used to obtain new tokens while the account remains active. | Implement short-lived access tokens with rotating refresh tokens and server-side revocable sessions or a user token-version check; revoke sessions on password change, compromise report, and deactivation; protect browser token storage and monitor unusual login/access patterns. |

## Out of scope

- Production deployment and operation on a public network, including chain selection, validator/sequencer behavior, finality, MEV, bridge risk, gas economics, and chain-specific replay protections. Public-chain front-running is identified above as a threat to assess before deployment, not as a claim about a current public deployment.
- Off-chain sensor/data-source integrity: the contracts can store submitted values, timestamps, and hashes, but this model does not establish that temperature, handling, location, or other physical-world observations are truthful or untampered before submission.

## Future work

- Define and test a production key-management, multisig, timelock, and emergency-recovery design.
- Add independent buyer/purchase evidence and Sybil/collusion monitoring while minimizing personal data exposure.
- Establish inspector independence, second-review rules, evidence retention, and an appeals process.
- Add chain-to-MongoDB integrity monitoring and recovery drills beyond retrying known `PendingSync` records.
- Conduct a public-network threat assessment and review transaction ordering, chain-ID/replay handling, and privacy before deployment.
- Validate score thresholds and collusion scenarios with ethically collected real-world evidence; keep simulations clearly distinguished from observed outcomes.
